# Nursing Artificial Intelligence Competency Framework

Below is a formal **AACN-style, competency-based AI framework** that can serve as a curriculum crosswalk, faculty-development blueprint, and basis for assessment.

The framework is **proposed rather than AACN-authored or AACN-endorsed**. Its structure follows the logic of the AACN Essentials: competencies should be observable, measurable, developmental, transferable across contexts, and demonstrated through performance rather than knowledge alone. AACN explicitly states that competence develops progressively and that knowledge by itself does not demonstrate competence. AACN also recommends integrating competencies across curricula with increasing complexity and using performance-based assessment.

The framework also treats AI as a cross-cutting capability rather than solely an informatics topic. That is consistent with AACN's Domain 8 definition, which includes use of information and communication technologies and informatics processes to gather data, support decision-making, and improve safe, high-quality care, while the broader Essentials simultaneously emphasize clinical judgment, ethics, evidence-based practice, quality, systems thinking, professionalism, and leadership.

## Level definitions

| Level | Expected relationship to AI |
|---|---|
| **Entry** | Safely uses and critically interprets AI-enabled tools within generalist nursing practice. |
| **Advanced** | Evaluates, integrates, monitors, and leads AI use within advanced practice and healthcare systems. |
| **PhD** | Generates, evaluates, and translates knowledge concerning AI, data science, and human-AI systems. |
| **Faculty** | Designs learning, models responsible practice, and assesses learners' AI competence while maintaining pedagogical and professional accountability. |

For the PhD level, this framework uses the separate AACN research-doctorate expectations because the Essentials state that they do not directly govern research-focused PhD preparation. AACN describes PhD preparation as focused on creating, translating, and communicating new knowledge and preparing graduates to develop science and steward the discipline.

# Domain 1 — AI Foundations and Literacy

## Domain competency

**Explain the concepts, capabilities, limitations, and appropriate roles of artificial intelligence and related computational technologies in nursing, health care, education, and scholarship.**

| Entry | Advanced | PhD | Faculty |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AI1.1-E** Distinguishes AI, machine learning, predictive analytics, generative AI, large language models, natural-language processing, automation, and conventional clinical decision-support systems at a foundational level. | **AI1.1-A** Differentiates major AI approaches sufficiently to evaluate their applicability to clinical, operational, population-health, or leadership problems. | **AI1.1-P** Explains conceptual and methodological foundations of AI approaches relevant to the individual's program of research. | **AI1.1-F** Teaches AI concepts at an appropriate level of complexity for the learner population and professional role. |
| **AI1.2-E** Explains that AI outputs are generated from computational patterns and data rather than human understanding, professional accountability, or clinical judgment. | **AI1.2-A** Explains how model development, training data, validation, deployment environment, and workflow influence performance. | **AI1.2-P** Critiques assumptions underlying AI models and their relationship to prediction, explanation, inference, and knowledge generation. | **AI1.2-F** Corrects common learner misconceptions about AI, including anthropomorphism, determinism, and assumptions of objectivity. |
| **AI1.3-E** Identifies common limitations including inaccurate outputs, hallucination, incomplete context, automation bias, outdated information, and overconfidence. | **AI1.3-A** Analyzes sources of model failure including dataset shift, poor calibration, limited external validation, weak data quality, and context mismatch. | **AI1.3-P** Evaluates theoretical and methodological limitations of AI approaches used in nursing and health research. | **AI1.3-F** Designs learning activities that require learners to identify AI capabilities, limitations, and uncertainty. |
| **AI1.4-E** Recognizes common locations in which AI may be embedded in contemporary nursing practice. | **AI1.4-A** Maps AI functions within clinical workflows, organizational systems, and health information infrastructure. | **AI1.4-P** Examines AI as part of a broader sociotechnical system rather than as an isolated algorithm. | **AI1.4-F** Maintains sufficient current knowledge to revise course content as AI technologies and evidence evolve. |

### Suggested assessment methods

| Entry | Advanced | PhD | Faculty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concept map; case identification exercise; short oral explanation; AI-literacy OSCE station. | Technology-appraisal brief; workflow mapping exercise; model-performance interpretation exercise. | Methods critique; doctoral seminar presentation; qualifying-exam question; research-protocol defense. | Teaching demonstration; AI concept module; peer review of instructional materials; faculty portfolio. |

# Domain 2 — AI Interaction and Effective Use

## Domain competency

**Use AI-enabled technologies purposefully, effectively, transparently, and within appropriate professional boundaries.**

| Entry | Advanced | PhD | Faculty |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AI2.1-E** Selects an appropriate approved AI tool for a defined, low-risk nursing or learning task. | **AI2.1-A** Selects AI tools according to clinical, operational, population, leadership, or analytic requirements rather than convenience alone. | **AI2.1-P** Selects AI or computational methods appropriate to the research question and distinguishes them from situations requiring other quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods. | **AI2.1-F** Selects AI tools for teaching based on educational purpose, learner level, privacy, accessibility, evidence, and institutional requirements. |
| **AI2.2-E** Constructs sufficiently clear prompts or inputs containing relevant context, constraints, and desired output characteristics. | **AI2.2-A** Designs structured human-AI workflows that incorporate verification, expert review, escalation, and documentation. | **AI2.2-P** Develops reproducible workflows for AI-assisted analysis, synthesis, coding, simulation, or research support as appropriate. | **AI2.2-F** Teaches learners how to structure interactions with AI without reducing AI competence to prompt-writing alone. |
| **AI2.3-E** Iteratively refines AI-generated output while preserving independent nursing reasoning. | **AI2.3-A** Integrates AI into workflow without creating inappropriate dependence or loss of professional oversight. | **AI2.3-P** Documents AI-assisted research processes sufficiently to support transparency and reproducibility. | **AI2.3-F** Models transparent AI use in teaching, scholarship, and course development. |
| **AI2.4-E** Distinguishes appropriate assistance from inappropriate delegation of nursing judgment or academic work. | **AI2.4-A** Establishes boundaries for tasks that may be automated, augmented, or retained under direct human control. | **AI2.4-P** Evaluates whether AI adds scientific value rather than methodological novelty alone. | **AI2.4-F** Distinguishes pedagogically productive AI use from use that substitutes for learning essential disciplinary reasoning. |

### Suggested assessment methods

| Entry | Advanced | PhD | Faculty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structured AI task; prompt/output portfolio with rationale; simulation exercise; reflective annotation of revisions. | Workflow-design project; implementation simulation; case-based technology selection; practice portfolio. | Reproducible analytic notebook or protocol; methods justification; dissertation proposal component. | AI-integrated lesson plan; teaching demonstration; assignment redesign; faculty AI-use disclosure statement. |

# Domain 3 — Critical Appraisal and Human Judgment

## Domain competency

**Critically evaluate AI-generated information and recommendations using nursing knowledge, evidence, contextual information, clinical reasoning, and human judgment.**

Clinical judgment is central in the Essentials and is defined as a process integrating nursing knowledge, evidence, critical thinking, and clinical reasoning to interpret information and make decisions.

| Entry | Advanced | PhD | Faculty |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AI3.1-E** Verifies factual or clinical claims generated by AI against appropriate authoritative sources. | **AI3.1-A** Critically appraises AI-supported recommendations using clinical evidence, population relevance, and professional expertise. | **AI3.1-P** Evaluates the validity of knowledge claims derived from AI-based research or analyses. | **AI3.1-F** Teaches explicit verification strategies rather than relying solely on warnings that AI may be inaccurate. |
| **AI3.2-E** Identifies inaccurate, internally inconsistent, unsupported, fabricated, or clinically inappropriate output. | **AI3.2-A** Interprets performance characteristics such as sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, discrimination, calibration, and relevant clinical outcomes. | **AI3.2-P** Identifies methodological problems such as leakage, overfitting, weak validation, inappropriate labels, inadequate comparison groups, and nonrepresentative datasets. | **AI3.2-F** Creates cases in which plausible AI outputs contain errors, omissions, or misleading recommendations that learners must identify. |
| **AI3.3-E** Compares AI recommendations with patient assessment findings and explains discrepancies. | **AI3.3-A** Determines when clinical context justifies accepting, modifying, rejecting, or escalating an AI-generated recommendation. | **AI3.3-P** Distinguishes predictive accuracy from causality, explanation, clinical utility, and scientific significance. | **AI3.3-F** Assesses the learner's reasoning process rather than only the correctness of the final answer. |
| **AI3.4-E** Maintains accountability for nursing decisions regardless of whether AI contributed to the decision process. | **AI3.4-A** Designs human oversight and escalation mechanisms for high-consequence AI-supported decisions. | **AI3.4-P** Defends or challenges AI-derived scientific claims using methodological and disciplinary reasoning. | **AI3.4-F** Models calibrated trust by neither uncritically accepting nor categorically rejecting AI-generated information. |
| **AI3.5-E** Communicates uncertainty when AI-derived information cannot be adequately verified. | **AI3.5-A** Communicates model limitations and uncertainty to clinicians, leaders, patients, and other stakeholders. | **AI3.5-P** Communicates uncertainty, assumptions, and limitations in scholarly dissemination. | **AI3.5-F** Designs assessments that require learners to articulate uncertainty and limits of evidence. |

### Suggested assessment methods

| Entry | Advanced | PhD | Faculty |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI-error detection OSCE; source-verification exercise; think-aloud clinical reasoning; case comparison. | Critical appraisal paper; model-performance interpretation; implementation decision memo; simulation with conflicting AI/clinical data. | Manuscript critique; peer review exercise; dissertation defense; reproducibility audit. | AI-error teaching case; learner reasoning rubric; observed teaching; assessment-design review. |

# Domain 4 — Ethics, Privacy, Equity, and Professionalism

## Domain competency

**Apply ethical, legal, privacy, security, equity, and professional principles to the selection, use, evaluation, and governance of AI.**

AACN's ethics framework incorporates autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice. The Essentials also frame professionalism as involving accountability and professional identity.

| Entry | Advanced | PhD | Faculty |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AI4.1-E** Protects protected health information, confidential information, credentials, and other sensitive data when interacting with AI systems. | **AI4.1-A** Evaluates institutional AI use in relation to privacy, security, data governance, access, and organizational policy. | **AI4.1-P** Incorporates privacy, consent, data governance, secondary data use, and responsible research principles into AI-related research. | **AI4.1-F** Protects patient, learner, faculty, research, and institutional information when using AI in education or scholarship. |
| **AI4.2-E** Uses institutionally approved tools and follows applicable professional and organizational policies. | **AI4.2-A** Contributes nursing expertise to AI governance, procurement, policy, or oversight processes. | **AI4.2-P** Evaluates ethical implications of dataset construction, algorithm development, dissemination, and deployment. | **AI4.2-F** Communicates clear and consistent expectations for acceptable AI use in coursework and assessment. |
| **AI4.3-E** Recognizes that AI systems may produce differential or unfair effects across patient groups. | **AI4.3-A** Evaluates subgroup performance, differential impact, access barriers, and potential inequitable consequences. | **AI4.3-P** Investigates sampling, measurement, historical, label, and algorithmic sources of bias and inequity. | **AI4.3-F** Teaches learners to evaluate AI through ethics, justice, access, and equity lenses. |
| **AI4.4-E** Identifies situations in which transparency about AI use is professionally appropriate. | **AI4.4-A** Develops organizational expectations for transparency, documentation, oversight, and accountability. | **AI4.4-P** Transparently reports AI contributions to research and scholarly products in accordance with applicable standards. | **AI4.4-F** Models transparent disclosure of relevant AI use in teaching and scholarly work. |
| **AI4.5-E** Escalates suspected unsafe, discriminatory, privacy-compromising, or professionally inappropriate AI use. | **AI4.5-A** Leads or participates in review of ethical and safety concerns related to AI systems. | **AI4.5-P** Examines structural and sociotechnical mechanisms through which AI may create or perpetuate harm. | **AI4.5-F** Creates psychologically safe opportunities for learners to discuss ethical uncertainty and inappropriate AI use. |

### Suggested assessment methods

| Entry | Advanced | PhD | Faculty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Privacy scenario; ethics case analysis; bias-detection case; professionalism reflection. | Governance memo; algorithmic impact assessment; ethics committee simulation; subgroup-analysis exercise. | IRB protocol; ethics analysis; bias/fairness methods plan; manuscript transparency audit. | Syllabus AI policy; ethics teaching case; assessment of student disclosure practices; faculty portfolio. |

# Domain 5 — Person-Centered Communication and Partnership

## Domain competency

**Use AI in ways that preserve person-centered care, therapeutic relationships, communication, autonomy, dignity, accessibility, and meaningful human participation in healthcare decisions.**

AACN defines person-centered care as holistic, individualized, respectful, compassionate, coordinated, and evidence-based.

| Entry | Advanced | PhD | Faculty |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AI5.1-E** Uses AI-generated communication only after verifying and adapting it to the patient's clinical context, preferences, language, culture, and health literacy. | **AI5.1-A** Designs AI-supported workflows that preserve shared decision-making and meaningful clinician-patient interaction. | **AI5.1-P** Investigates how AI affects communication, trust, autonomy, relationships, access, and patient experience. | **AI5.1-F** Teaches learners to adapt AI output to the individual rather than using generic output as final patient communication. |
| **AI5.2-E** Explains AI-supported information in understandable language without overstating certainty or authority. | **AI5.2-A** Communicates AI-derived risk, uncertainty, and recommendations in ways appropriate for patients, families, clinicians, and leaders. | **AI5.2-P** Develops or evaluates methods for communicating AI-derived evidence to varied audiences. | **AI5.2-F** Models communication about AI limitations, uncertainty, and appropriate human oversight. |
| **AI5.3-E** Incorporates patient preferences and values when AI-generated recommendations are considered. | **AI5.3-A** Ensures patient and frontline stakeholder perspectives inform AI selection, implementation, and evaluation. | **AI5.3-P** Incorporates stakeholder and community perspectives into AI-related research when appropriate. | **AI5.3-F** Integrates patient and community perspectives into AI teaching cases and simulations. |
| **AI5.4-E** Recognizes when technology use may impair presence, empathy, communication, or therapeutic relationship. | **AI5.4-A** Evaluates whether efficiency gains from AI alter relational quality, access, burden, or patient experience. | **AI5.4-P** Studies intended and unintended relational consequences of AI-enabled care. | **AI5.4-F** Teaches students to balance technological efficiency with nursing presence and relational care. |

### Suggested assessment methods

| Entry | Advanced | PhD | Faculty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standardized-patient encounter; revision of AI-generated education; communication OSCE; patient-preference case. | Stakeholder-engagement plan; implementation simulation; communication protocol; patient-experience evaluation. | Qualitative or mixed-methods proposal; community-engagement plan; dissemination exercise. | Teaching simulation; case redesign; standardized learner encounter; review of patient-facing educational assignments. |

# Domain 6 — Safety, Quality, and Systems Integration

## Domain competency

**Evaluate and improve the safety, quality, workflow integration, effectiveness, and system-level consequences of AI-enabled technologies.**

AACN defines quality and safety in terms of minimizing risk through system effectiveness and individual performance and defines systems-based practice as responding to and leading within complex healthcare systems.

| Entry | Advanced | PhD | Faculty |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AI6.1-E** Recognizes when an AI-generated recommendation, summary, alert, or prediction may create a safety risk. | **AI6.1-A** Conducts prospective and retrospective evaluation of AI-related clinical and operational risks. | **AI6.1-P** Designs studies that evaluate AI safety, effectiveness, implementation, or unintended consequences. | **AI6.1-F** Uses authentic cases to teach AI-related safety and quality problems. |
| **AI6.2-E** Escalates suspected AI-related errors through appropriate clinical and organizational channels. | **AI6.2-A** Develops escalation, override, downtime, contingency, and monitoring procedures for AI-enabled systems. | **AI6.2-P** Investigates mechanisms of AI-related failure at algorithmic, human, organizational, and system levels. | **AI6.2-F** Assesses whether learners can recognize and appropriately respond to unsafe AI output. |
| **AI6.3-E** Recognizes automation bias, alert fatigue, overreliance, and loss of situational awareness as potential hazards. | **AI6.3-A** Evaluates human factors, cognitive workload, workflow disruption, deskilling, and alert burden associated with AI. | **AI6.3-P** Applies human-factors, implementation-science, or sociotechnical frameworks to AI research. | **AI6.3-F** Designs simulation experiences that include automation bias, uncertainty, and conflicting information. |
| **AI6.4-E** Uses AI-assisted workflows without bypassing required nursing assessment or safety checks. | **AI6.4-A** Measures process, outcome, balancing, safety, equity, and workflow effects following AI implementation. | **AI6.4-P** Develops or evaluates outcome measures that capture clinically meaningful effects rather than technical performance alone. | **AI6.4-F** Ensures AI-enabled educational activities reinforce rather than bypass essential nursing processes. |
| **AI6.5-E** Reports technology-related safety concerns as part of a culture of quality improvement. | **AI6.5-A** Participates in root-cause analysis and quality improvement when AI contributes to adverse events or near misses. | **AI6.5-P** Produces evidence that informs system redesign, implementation, policy, or deimplementation. | **AI6.5-F** Integrates AI into quality-and-safety education across clinical courses rather than treating it as an isolated topic. |

### Suggested assessment methods

| Entry | Advanced | PhD | Faculty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simulation; near-miss case; incident-escalation scenario; clinical judgment OSCE. | Failure-mode analysis; implementation plan; QI project; root-cause-analysis exercise; monitoring dashboard. | Implementation-science proposal; safety study; health-services research project; dissertation chapter. | Simulation design; learner performance rubric; curriculum mapping; debriefing evaluation. |

# Domain 7 — Evidence, Scholarship, and Knowledge Generation

## Domain competency

**Appraise, generate, translate, and disseminate evidence concerning AI in accordance with the individual's nursing role and level of preparation.**

AACN describes evidence-based practice as integration of current evidence, clinician expertise, and patient preferences while also examining how evidence is created and what harms it may produce. The PhD document explicitly identifies data science and analytic advances as requiring integration into nurse-scientist education.

| Entry | Advanced | PhD | Faculty |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AI7.1-E** Distinguishes AI-generated content from peer-reviewed evidence, primary data, authoritative clinical guidance, and expert judgment. | **AI7.1-A** Critically appraises AI development, validation, implementation, and outcomes studies relevant to practice. | **AI7.1-P** Formulates nursing-relevant research questions for which AI or data-science methods are scientifically appropriate. | **AI7.1-F** Teaches learners to distinguish AI-generated synthesis from primary evidence and authoritative sources. |
| **AI7.2-E** Verifies references, quotations, statistics, and evidence claims provided by generative AI. | **AI7.2-A** Evaluates population fit, external validity, comparator selection, clinically meaningful outcomes, and subgroup effects. | **AI7.2-P** Designs rigorous AI-related research using appropriate development, validation, implementation, qualitative, mixed, or comparative methods. | **AI7.2-F** Designs assignments requiring traceable sourcing and verification of AI-assisted scholarly work. |
| **AI7.3-E** Uses AI appropriately to support—not replace—literature exploration, organization, or writing when permitted. | **AI7.3-A** Uses AI-assisted evidence synthesis or analytics with appropriate validation and methodological oversight. | **AI7.3-P** Ensures reproducibility through documentation of data provenance, preprocessing, analytic decisions, model versions, and validation procedures. | **AI7.3-F** Establishes expectations for disclosure, citation, authorship, verification, and scholarly integrity in AI-assisted work. |
| **AI7.4-E** Identifies circumstances in which AI-generated evidence summaries are incomplete or misleading. | **AI7.4-A** Translates AI evidence into practice or policy while explicitly considering evidence quality and implementation context. | **AI7.4-P** Generates and communicates new knowledge concerning AI, nursing, health, education, or healthcare systems. | **AI7.4-F** Evaluates learner scholarship for evidence quality and reasoning rather than assuming polished AI-generated prose reflects competence. |
| **AI7.5-E** Transparently acknowledges AI assistance when required. | **AI7.5-A** Contributes nursing expertise to interdisciplinary AI scholarship and evidence translation. | **AI7.5-P** Defends AI-derived knowledge claims against methodological and disciplinary critique. | **AI7.5-F** Models responsible AI use in scholarly products and research mentorship. |

# Recommended proficiency language for curriculum mapping

For your internal curriculum crosswalk, I would avoid merely marking a competency as “covered.” Instead, use four developmental states:

| Designation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Introduced** | Learner encounters the concept and can explain foundational principles. |
| **Developing** | Learner performs the competency with guidance in relatively controlled contexts. |
| **Demonstrated** | Learner independently demonstrates the competency in an authentic or high-fidelity context. |
| **Integrated** | Learner transfers and combines the competency across settings, problems, and professional responsibilities. |

This is much closer to the AACN competency-based model than a conventional content checklist because AACN emphasizes repeated demonstration in multiple authentic contexts rather than a “one and done” approach.

# Minimum end-of-program expectations

For **Entry-level graduates**, I would require demonstrated competence in AI1 through AI5 and AI6 safety behaviors, with foundational achievement in AI7 and AI8.

For **Advanced-level graduates**, I would require independent competence across all eight domains, with particular emphasis on evaluation, implementation, governance, systems impact, and leadership.

For **PhD graduates**, I would require advanced achievement in AI1, AI3, AI4, AI7, and AI8, with depth in AI2, AI5, and AI6 determined partly by the student's research trajectory. AACN's PhD framework explicitly anticipates nurse scientists participating in interdisciplinary teams using informatics, healthcare data repositories, data science, and new analytic approaches.

For **faculty**, I would require two simultaneous forms of competence: competence appropriate to the nursing content or level they teach, and **pedagogical AI competence**—the ability to design, facilitate, model, and assess AI-related learning.

# Implications for the faculty training video series

This matrix gives you a clean way to move away from generic “AI training” and make every video competency-driven. Each module can follow a consistent structure:

**Competency → authentic nursing problem → AI use or output → risk/limitation → faculty teaching strategy → learner performance task → assessment criterion.**

For example, instead of a video entitled **“AI Hallucinations,”** the faculty module could be framed as **“Teaching Students to Verify AI-Generated Clinical Information.”** The performance expectation becomes observable: the learner must detect an unsupported claim, locate an authoritative source, reconcile the discrepancy, explain the clinical significance, and document or communicate the corrected information.

Similarly, instead of **“Prompt Engineering,”** the learning target becomes **“Use AI effectively while preserving nursing reasoning and accountability.”**

That framing is much more compatible with competency-based nursing education because it focuses on what the learner can reliably **do**, not merely what content the faculty member presented.

A logical next step would be to turn this framework into a **curriculum-crosswalk spreadsheet** with one row per subcompetency, columns for AACN domain alignment, Entry/Advanced/PhD/Faculty level, existing course(s), Introduced/Developing/Demonstrated/Integrated status, assessment method, faculty-development need, and corresponding training-video module.
