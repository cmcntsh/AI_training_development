# Proposed Nursing AI Competency Framework

Yes. I would treat this as a **leveled AI competency framework for nursing**, rather than a stand-alone “AI course.” That approach is highly consistent with the AACN Essentials: competencies are intended to be observable and measurable, to develop progressively, and to be demonstrated across multiple contexts rather than addressed once in a curriculum. AACN also explicitly recommends scaffolding competencies across curricula with increasing complexity and using performance-based assessment.

There is also a strong conceptual home for AI within the Essentials. Domain 8 already expects nurses to use information and communication technologies and informatics processes to gather data, support decision-making, expand knowledge, and improve safe and efficient healthcare. At the same time, AI cannot reasonably be confined to Domain 8: it intersects clinical judgment, evidence-based practice, ethics, quality and safety, professionalism, systems practice, scholarship, and leadership. For example, AACN defines clinical judgment as integrating nursing knowledge, evidence, critical thinking, and clinical reasoning to interpret information and make decisions.

What follows is therefore a **proposed framework derived from the AACN documents**, not an AACN-published set of AI competencies.

## Proposed Nursing AI Competency Framework

I would define eight longitudinal competency domains.

| AI competency domain | Core question |
|---|---|
| **1. AI Foundations & Literacy** | What is AI, what can it do, and what can it not do? |
| **2. AI Interaction & Effective Use** | How do I appropriately use AI tools to accomplish nursing work? |
| **3. Critical Appraisal & Human Judgment** | How do I determine whether an AI output should be trusted or acted upon? |
| **4. Ethics, Privacy, Equity & Professionalism** | Should AI be used here, and what responsibilities accompany its use? |
| **5. Person-Centered Communication & Partnership** | How can AI support rather than undermine human relationships and patient agency? |
| **6. Safety, Quality & Systems Integration** | How do AI systems affect workflows, outcomes, safety, and healthcare systems? |
| **7. Evidence, Scholarship & Knowledge Generation** | How do we evaluate, study, develop, and generate evidence concerning AI? |
| **8. Lifelong Learning & AI Leadership** | How do nurses adapt responsibly as AI changes? |

The **same domains persist across the educational trajectory**, while expectations become progressively more complex. That mirrors AACN's Level 1 → Level 2 model: advanced-level competencies broaden the context, complexity, and autonomy expected of the learner.

## 1. Entry-Level Professional Nurse

The goal at entry level should **not** be to make the nurse an AI expert. It should be to produce an **AI-literate, appropriately skeptical, safe user of AI-enabled healthcare systems who retains responsibility for nursing judgment**.

This fits AACN's expectation that entry-level graduates are generalists who demonstrate Level 1 competencies across populations, settings, and spheres of care.

### Overall competency

**Uses AI-enabled technologies appropriately and safely in nursing practice while critically evaluating AI-generated information, protecting patient interests and information, recognizing limitations and potential bias, and maintaining human accountability for nursing judgment and care.**

### Knowledge

| Area | Entry-level nurse should know |
|---|---|
| AI concepts | Basic meanings of AI, machine learning, generative AI, predictive algorithms, natural-language processing, clinical decision support, automation, and large language models. |
| How AI works | AI identifies statistical patterns rather than “knowing” in the human sense; outputs are probabilistic and dependent on training data, input data, context, and system design. |
| Limitations | Hallucination/fabrication, automation bias, incomplete information, poor calibration, outdated knowledge, brittleness, overconfidence, and context limitations. |
| Data | Why data quality, representativeness, provenance, missingness, and documentation influence AI performance. |
| Clinical judgment | Difference between AI-generated recommendation and professional nursing judgment. |
| Privacy/security | Risks of entering protected, confidential, proprietary, or personally identifiable information into unapproved systems. |
| Bias/equity | Algorithms can reproduce or amplify disparities present in data, processes, or system design. |
| Professional responsibility | The nurse remains responsible for appropriate assessment, verification, documentation, escalation, and communication. |
| Evidence | AI-generated information is not automatically evidence and must be evaluated against reliable sources. |

This emphasis is particularly compatible with the Essentials' framing of evidence-based practice as integration of research evidence, clinician expertise, and patient preferences and values, while also questioning whose perspectives generated the evidence and what harms it may create.

### Skills

The entry-level nurse should be able to formulate a clear AI query for a low-risk professional task; recognize when AI is embedded in an EHR, monitor, predictive score, documentation system, or clinical decision-support tool; verify AI-generated factual or clinical information against appropriate sources; compare an AI recommendation with assessment findings and nursing knowledge; identify obvious hallucinations, inconsistencies, unsupported claims, inappropriate recommendations, or missing context; avoid inappropriate disclosure of patient information; explain AI-assisted information to patients without presenting the algorithm as an unquestionable authority; document AI use when institutional policy requires it; and escalate concerning AI outputs or suspected system errors.

### Abilities and attitudes

The desired disposition is **calibrated trust rather than enthusiasm or avoidance**. Entry-level nurses should demonstrate curiosity, humility, willingness to verify, respect for privacy, commitment to patient autonomy, sensitivity to inequitable effects, and willingness to override or challenge technology when it conflicts with assessment findings or patient interests.

That matters because AACN explicitly connects professional nursing to ethical principles including autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice.

### Example performance expectations

A student presented with an AI-generated patient summary should be able to identify missing or inaccurate information before using it during handoff. Given an AI-generated patient education document, the student should verify accuracy, adjust reading level and cultural/contextual appropriateness, and individualize it to the patient's goals. Given a deterioration-risk score that conflicts with bedside assessment, the student should explain why the score does not replace clinical assessment and describe the appropriate escalation pathway.

## 2. Advanced-Level Nurse

Here I would include DNP/APRN, leadership, informatics, population health, policy, education, and other advanced nursing roles, recognizing that the precise depth varies by specialty.

The transition is essentially:

**Entry:** “Can I use and interpret AI safely?”

**Advanced:** “Can I evaluate, integrate, supervise, improve, and lead the use of AI within care systems?”

### Overall competency

**Integrates, evaluates, and leads the responsible use of AI-enabled technologies within advanced nursing practice and healthcare systems, using evidence, clinical expertise, patient values, quality-improvement methods, and systems thinking to determine whether AI improves care.**

### Knowledge

| Area | Advanced nurse should know |
|---|---|
| AI architecture | More detailed understanding of predictive versus generative systems, model training/validation, inputs/outputs, performance metrics, and model lifecycle. |
| Evaluation | Sensitivity, specificity, PPV/NPV, discrimination, calibration, external validation, subgroup performance, and clinically meaningful outcomes. |
| Human factors | Automation bias, alert fatigue, workflow effects, deskilling, cognitive load, human-AI interaction, and unintended consequences. |
| Implementation | Sociotechnical systems, workflow redesign, implementation science, quality improvement, governance, and monitoring. |
| Data governance | Data quality, interoperability, provenance, access, security, secondary use, and institutional governance. |
| Regulatory/legal issues | Institutional policy, professional scope/accountability, documentation, applicable regulatory requirements, and vendor governance. |
| Equity | Algorithmic bias, subgroup evaluation, digital access barriers, fairness concepts, and differential impact on populations. |
| Evidence | Critical appraisal of AI validation and implementation studies. |
| Economics | Resource implications, efficiency claims, opportunity costs, and downstream consequences. |

### Skills

Advanced nurses should be able to critically evaluate a published AI study; interpret model-performance measures rather than relying on an abstract's conclusions; determine whether the study population resembles their intended patient population; assess subgroup performance and potential inequitable consequences; compare AI-supported care with existing workflows; participate in technology procurement and governance discussions; design or participate in a pilot implementation; create clinical safeguards and escalation procedures; monitor process, balancing, safety, and patient outcomes after implementation; conduct root-cause analysis when AI contributes to a safety event; and communicate with informaticians, data scientists, clinicians, administrators, patients, compliance personnel, and vendors.

### Abilities and attitudes

Advanced nurses need systems thinking, tolerance for ambiguity, willingness to challenge vendor or institutional claims, commitment to transparent implementation, intellectual humility, responsibility for downstream consequences, and active inclusion of patient and frontline-clinician perspectives.

This is directly compatible with AACN's Systems-Based Practice descriptor, which expects nurses to respond to and lead within complex healthcare systems, coordinating resources for safe and high-quality care.

### Example performance expectation

An advanced learner could be given a proposed AI sepsis-risk system and asked to make an implementation recommendation. The learner would examine validation evidence, population fit, clinical workflow, false-positive and false-negative consequences, subgroup performance, governance, patient safety safeguards, staff education, outcome measures, and post-deployment monitoring.

That is a much stronger competency assessment than “describe how AI may affect healthcare.”

## 3. PhD-Prepared Nurse Scientist

I would deliberately separate the PhD level from “Advanced,” just as you are proposing.

AACN itself says that the Essentials do **not directly apply** to preparation of nurse researchers in research-focused PhD programs, although Level 2 subcompetencies can support doctoral core preparation.

The PhD document provides the more appropriate frame: PhD graduates should create, translate, and communicate new knowledge, develop nursing science, and steward the discipline. It also specifically identifies the evolution of **data information science and precision health science** as requiring new knowledge paradigms within nursing science and nurse-scientist education.

### Overall competency

**Generates, evaluates, and translates rigorous knowledge concerning AI, data science, and human-AI systems relevant to nursing and health, while ensuring methodological rigor, reproducibility, ethical integrity, disciplinary relevance, and equitable impact.**

### Knowledge

| Area | PhD-prepared nurse should know |
|---|---|
| AI/data-science foundations | Conceptual foundations sufficient to collaborate meaningfully with statisticians, computer scientists, informaticians, and engineers. |
| Methods | Supervised/unsupervised learning concepts, NLP, generative models, model validation, causal versus predictive inference, measurement, and appropriate statistical methods. |
| Research design | AI algorithm development studies, validation studies, pragmatic evaluations, implementation studies, human-factors studies, mixed-methods research, and comparative effectiveness research. |
| Reproducibility | Dataset provenance, preprocessing, feature construction, documentation, versioning, reporting standards, reproducibility, and external validation. |
| Bias/fairness | Sampling bias, measurement bias, label bias, historical bias, fairness metrics, subgroup analysis, and sociotechnical determinants of algorithmic harm. |
| Epistemology | How AI-derived knowledge claims relate to nursing epistemology, theory, explanation, prediction, causality, and disciplinary knowledge. |
| Research ethics | Consent, secondary data use, data ownership, privacy, community impact, accountability, transparency, and responsible research conduct. |
| Translation | Moving AI discoveries into practice, implementation, policy, and population health. |

The PhD document already anticipates this trajectory. It notes that technology and data-science skills will allow PhD nurses to contribute on interprofessional teams developing new analytic approaches in outcomes and translational research.

### Skills

The PhD nurse scientist should be able to formulate nursing-relevant questions for which AI methods are appropriate; determine when conventional statistics, causal methods, qualitative methods, or AI are more appropriate than one another; collaborate substantively on AI/data-science research; critique model development and validation; detect leakage, overfitting, inappropriate outcome construction, weak external validation, or misleading performance claims; design studies examining human-AI interaction and implementation; investigate differential effects on populations; conduct or supervise reproducible analyses; publish AI-related nursing research using appropriate reporting standards; translate findings to practice and policy; and contribute nursing perspectives to interdisciplinary AI research teams.

### Abilities and attitudes

The central scholarly disposition is **epistemic rigor**: curiosity about AI without methodological novelty-seeking. The PhD nurse should prioritize important nursing questions over fashionable methods and should be willing to conclude that AI adds no value when evidence supports that conclusion.

That aligns with AACN's conception of disciplinary stewardship: generating knowledge, defending knowledge claims against criticism, conserving important knowledge, and transforming that knowledge through dissemination.

AACN further expects PhD graduates and faculty to ask research-worthy questions, formulate appropriate investigative strategies, conduct and evaluate research, communicate results, generate knowledge for policy, and develop new knowledge relevant to nursing practice and education.

## 4. Nursing Faculty

This is the level that is often missed.

Faculty do not merely need the student competency **plus a little more AI knowledge**. They need an additional, qualitatively different competence:

> **AI pedagogical competence.**

A faculty member must know enough about AI to determine **what students need to learn, how they should learn it, how their competence can be demonstrated, and when AI use undermines rather than facilitates learning.**

AACN's competency-based framework makes this particularly important because faculty are responsible for creating repeated learning opportunities, observing performance, judging competence against explicit criteria, and giving feedback.

### Overall faculty competency

**Designs, facilitates, models, and evaluates learning experiences that prepare nursing students to use, critique, and govern AI responsibly, while appropriately incorporating AI into teaching and assessment without compromising learning, professional formation, academic integrity, patient safety, or faculty responsibility.**

I would divide faculty competence into **two layers**.

| Faculty layer | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **AI competence for nursing** | Faculty possess at least the AI competency appropriate to the learners and clinical/scholarly domain they teach. |
| **AI competence for teaching nursing** | Faculty know how to teach, assess, model, and continually update those competencies. |

AACN's PhD document makes a parallel argument for doctoral faculty: faculty should have expertise aligned with what they teach, model scholarship, expose students to multiple perspectives, challenge students' reasoning, and provide effective mentoring. Faculty are also expected to model discovery and help students learn from the actual process of knowledge development and dissemination.

### Faculty knowledge

Faculty should understand the core AI concepts expected of their students plus AI's implications for learning, assessment, academic integrity, clinical education, simulation, research, copyright/intellectual property, privacy, accessibility, misinformation, and professional identity.

They should understand the distinction among using AI as a tutor, brainstorming partner, simulator, writing aid, information retrieval tool, clinical decision support tool, research tool, and substitute for cognitive work.

Most importantly, faculty should understand **which cognitive work students must perform themselves to become nurses**.

For example, a student may appropriately use AI to generate five alternative patient-education explanations. The same student probably should not outsource the entire process of recognizing deterioration in a clinical reasoning assessment.

### Faculty skills

Faculty should be able to redesign assignments so that AI does not simply invalidate the assessment; write transparent AI-use expectations; create AI-inclusive and AI-restricted learning activities intentionally rather than reflexively; demonstrate responsible use and verification; construct cases containing plausible but erroneous AI output and ask learners to critique them; teach verification strategies rather than merely warning about “hallucinations”; design simulation incorporating AI-supported workflows; assess the learner's reasoning process as well as final answer; create rubrics for AI competencies; disclose their own pedagogical AI use appropriately; protect student and patient information when selecting tools; and update course material as technology changes.

### Faculty abilities and attitudes

Faculty need willingness to experiment, but also willingness to say no to inappropriate applications. They need comfort admitting uncertainty, intellectual humility, transparency, tolerance for changing technology, respect for learners' legitimate use of tools, and a commitment to assessing nursing competence rather than detecting AI use.

A particularly important faculty attitude is:

**The educational objective is not “students must use AI” or “students must not use AI.” The objective is that students learn when, why, and how AI use is appropriate.**

## The developmental progression in one table

| Dimension | Entry | Advanced | PhD | Faculty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Relationship to AI** | Safe user | Evaluator/integrator | Investigator/knowledge generator | Educator/model |
| **Primary question** | “Can I use this safely?” | “Should and how should we implement this?” | “What can we validly know about this?” | “How do students become competent at this?” |
| **AI literacy** | Explain basic concepts | Analyze system/model characteristics | Interrogate methodological assumptions | Teach concepts accurately |
| **Use** | Use approved tools for appropriate tasks | Integrate tools into advanced workflows | Use/develop AI methods in scholarship | Design AI-enabled learning |
| **Evaluation** | Verify individual outputs | Evaluate evidence and system performance | Generate validation evidence | Teach and assess evaluation |
| **Clinical judgment** | Do not substitute AI for judgment | Integrate AI with advanced judgment | Study human-AI decision making | Teach students to preserve judgment |
| **Ethics/equity** | Recognize privacy, bias, autonomy issues | Analyze/manage organizational risks | Investigate mechanisms and consequences | Create ethical educational practice |
| **Safety** | Detect and escalate unsafe output | Design safeguards and monitoring | Generate safety evidence | Teach safety through authentic cases |
| **Systems** | Understand AI within workflow | Lead implementation/governance | Study sociotechnical systems | Prepare students for AI-enabled systems |
| **Scholarship** | Distinguish AI output from evidence | Critically appraise AI evidence | Generate new AI-related science | Teach appropriate scholarly AI use |
| **Accountability** | Accountable for own use | Accountable for implementation/oversight | Accountable for scientific integrity | Accountable for learning and assessment |
| **Expected autonomy** | Guided | Independent/leadership | Scientific independence | Pedagogical leadership |

## A useful K–S–A/A shorthand

For your faculty-development videos, I think this can be condensed into a memorable framework:

**KNOW → USE → JUDGE → GOVERN → ADVANCE → TEACH**

**Entry nurses:** Know, Use, Judge.

**Advanced nurses:** Know, Use, Judge, Govern.

**PhD nurses:** Know, Judge, Investigate, Advance.

**Faculty:** Know, Use, Judge, Govern **and Teach**.

That preserves the difference between levels without implying that a PhD nurse simply needs to be “better at prompting” than an undergraduate.

## Competencies I would formally adopt

If your College wanted a relatively compact set of institutional AI competencies, I would propose the following wording:

| # | College-wide AI competency |
|---|---|
| **AI-1** | **Explain foundational AI concepts, capabilities, limitations, and their relevance to nursing and health.** |
| **AI-2** | **Use appropriate AI-enabled technologies effectively and transparently for nursing practice, education, scholarship, or leadership.** |
| **AI-3** | **Critically evaluate AI-generated information and recommendations using disciplinary knowledge, evidence, contextual information, and human judgment.** |
| **AI-4** | **Apply ethical, legal, privacy, security, professional, and equity principles when selecting or using AI.** |
| **AI-5** | **Integrate AI in ways that preserve person-centered care, communication, autonomy, compassion, and human accountability.** |
| **AI-6** | **Evaluate and improve the safety, quality, effectiveness, workflow implications, and population effects of AI-enabled systems.** |
| **AI-7** | **Contribute to the generation, appraisal, translation, or implementation of evidence concerning AI appropriate to one's educational and professional role.** |
| **AI-8** | **Engage in continuing learning and leadership as AI technologies, evidence, policy, and professional standards evolve.** |
| **AI-F1** | **Faculty: Design and facilitate educational experiences through which learners progressively develop AI competencies.** |
| **AI-F2** | **Faculty: Assess AI-related nursing competence through observable performance rather than knowledge recall alone.** |
| **AI-F3** | **Faculty: Model transparent, ethical, evidence-informed use of AI in teaching, clinical education, scholarship, and assessment.** |

The final three are intentionally faculty-specific.

## How I would translate this into your training videos

Rather than producing a generic sequence such as “What is ChatGPT?”, “Prompt engineering,” and “Ethics,” I would organize faculty training around **what faculty must enable students to do**.

A strong sequence would be:

1. **AI Literacy for Nurses — What Every Graduate Needs to Understand**
2. **Using AI Without Outsourcing Clinical Judgment**
3. **Hallucinations, Evidence, and Verification**
4. **Privacy, Confidentiality, Ethics, Bias, and Equity**
5. **AI and Person-Centered Nursing Care**
6. **AI in Clinical Decision Support, EHRs, and Healthcare Systems**
7. **Generative AI for Nursing Education: What Helps Learning and What Replaces Learning**
8. **Designing AI-Resilient and AI-Inclusive Assessments**
9. **Teaching Students to Critique AI Outputs**
10. **AI for Advanced Practice, Quality Improvement, and Leadership**
11. **AI for Nursing Scholarship and PhD Education**
12. **Faculty AI Competency: Modeling, Assessment, Governance, and Lifelong Learning**

I would make **#2 and #3 foundational**, rather than “prompt engineering.” Prompt construction is useful, but it is not the central professional competency. The professionally consequential skill is **knowing whether an output warrants trust and what action should follow**.

There is an especially strong rationale for this in the AACN documents. Informatics competency is described as foundational to all nursing practice, not limited to informatics specialists. And the PhD document explicitly anticipates nurses working at the intersection of informatics, technology, data repositories, analytic approaches, and interdisciplinary science.

## One additional distinction I would make for the College

It may be useful to explicitly separate **AI fluency** from **AI competency**.

**AI fluency** means someone can operate the technology.

**AI competency** means someone can use it appropriately in fulfillment of a professional role.

Someone can be excellent at prompting ChatGPT and still be incompetent in nursing AI practice if they cannot recognize fabricated evidence, protect PHI, detect automation bias, account for patient preferences, recognize subgroup harm, or determine when AI should not be used.

That distinction could become a central theme of your faculty development effort.

Finally, the PhD framework gives you a useful philosophical anchor for the highest level: nurse scientists are expected not merely to consume new technologies, but to **develop nursing science and steward the discipline**. AI competency at the PhD and faculty levels therefore needs to include the ability to challenge what AI optimizes, whose knowledge it represents, what outcomes it privileges, and whether it actually advances nursing's disciplinary goals—not simply whether the technology “works.”
